# Sneaky Business Tricks #1: why prices end in .99 (60 s vertical cartoon)

Part 1 of the "Sneaky Business Tricks" series. Same engine and fast style as the other shorts (camera change about
every 1.5 s, gags every 3-5 s, information on signs in the world, one-word captions, American narrator am_fenrir, speed 1.15),
set in Tony's world: Tony (recurring host) flips his burger from $5.00 to $4.99.

**Story.** $4.99 isn't $5, but your brain reads the first digit (the left-digit effect): $4.99 feels like $4. One cent
cheaper, a whole dollar in your head. In a famous catalog test the same dress sold at $34, $39 and $44; $39 sold
best, even beating $34. Where did 99 start? Nobody knows for sure: a popular legend says owners wanted cashiers to open
the register and make change (sepia 1880 flashback); 99-cent prices were in newspaper ads by 1880. Some fancy places
do the opposite: round prices, no nines; some even drop the dollar sign (part two). Is it a deal, or just a nine?

## Fact check

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| People weigh the left digit most ($4.99 feels much cheaper than $5.00) | ✅ Left-digit effect, many studies (strength varies; some replications fail) |
| Catalog test: same dress at $34 / $39 / $44, $39 sold best | ✅ Anderson & Simester (MIT / U. Chicago), reported in HBR 2003 "Mind Your Pricing Cues" |
| Origin of .99 unknown; cashier-theft story is a legend | ✅ Said as "a popular story" (Fordham's Robert Schindler: "legend has it") |
| 99-cent prices in ads by 1880 | ✅ Macy's ad in an 1880 New York Times (per Schindler) |
| Some fancy places use round prices / drop the $ sign | ✅ Hedged with "some"; evidence on round prices and quality is mixed |

All places are made up (Tony's Burgers, MAISON D'OR, The Daily Times).
